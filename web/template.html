What we excluded and why

  • Wedgewood Polls. The firm admitted on October 20 that it fabricated its polls. Its Kansas, Nebraska, Mississippi and Michigan numbers are not in the data.
  • A Decision Desk HQ poll list in Iowa, Michigan and Georgia contained entries that looked like test data (invented-sounding pollsters, placeholder links). None of them were used.
  • Duplicates. Where two aggregators listed the same poll with different dates, we kept one row.
  • Prediction markets and other models are shown for comparison only. Feeding them in would be circular, since markets already price in the public models.

Training data, in plain terms

This version learns one thing from history: how wrong late-cycle forecasts are. We use FiftyPlusOne's published out-of-sample forecasts for every House and Senate race from 2012 to 2024, made 15 days before each election, next to the actual results. That gives the size of the national miss in each of 7 cycles and the size of race-level misses in 149 competitive Senate contests.

The fundamentals coefficients (how much incumbency or state lean is worth) are set from the published literature in v0, not fitted. Fitting them needs state-level inputs for every race back to 2012, which is the first v1 task.

Methodology

Five steps, all in about 200 lines of Python. The browser re-runs step 4 when you move the slider.

  1. Fundamentals. Each state's expected margin before any polls: its partisan lean (Cook PVI, doubled to convert vote share into margin), plus the national generic-ballot margin, plus an incumbency bonus. 
    fundamentals = 2 × PVI + generic ballot + incumbency + candidate adjustment
incumbency   = +3.0 elected incumbent, +1.0 appointed (toward their party)
  2. Poll average. Each public poll since mid-September is weighted by age (half-life 14 days), sample type (likely voters 1.0, unreported 0.9, registered 0.8) and sponsor (campaign or party polls count half and are shifted 2 points against the sponsor). The sum of the weights is the effective number of polls.
  3. Blend. The two estimates are combined by inverse variance. A race with many fresh polls leans on polls; a race with none uses fundamentals alone. 
    poll variance  = 3.5² + 30 / effective polls
blend weight   = (1/poll var) / (1/poll var + 1/8²)
  4. Simulate. 40,000 elections. Each one draws a national error shared by every race (fat-tailed, standard deviation points, from the 7-cycle backtest), a regional error (1.5 points), and a race error (at least points, wider where polls are thin).
  5. Count. Republicans hold 31 seats that are not up; Democrats and independents hold 34. Republicans keep control with 50 or more, because the vice president breaks ties.

Special cases

  • Nebraska and South Dakota have no Democrat; the independent is the challenger. Osborn gets a +12 candidate adjustment in fundamentals because he ran far ahead of Nebraska's lean in 2024. Polls dominate either way.
  • Montana is a three-way race. The independent's fundamentals are reduced by the Democrat's polling share (about 17 points), because they split the same voters.
  • Alaska and Maine use ranked-choice voting. We use final-round numbers where a poll reports them.
  • Georgia and Mississippi require a majority. The model treats the November margin as decisive; a December runoff is not simulated.

Known limits of v0

  • Fundamentals coefficients are priors, not fitted on history.
  • Pollster house effects are not estimated; only sponsor bias is corrected.
  • The House is not modeled yet. It needs presidential results on the 2026 district lines in nine redrawn states.
  • Error sizes come from a more elaborate model, so the race error is inflated by 25% to stay honest about ours.

Issues & economy

The things voters say they care about, and why they reach this model through the national environment rather than as separate inputs.

Why inflation, gas prices and immigration are not separate model inputs

They are national numbers. In a given year every race sees the same CPI and the same gas price, so they can only explain the national swing, never why Ohio differs from Iowa. There are about seven comparable elections to learn that swing from, too few to estimate several separate coefficients without fitting noise.

Their effect is already in the model. Voters answer the generic-ballot question and the presidential approval question with prices, the border and enforcement in mind. Adding the raw economic series on top would count them twice.

The right test, on the v1 list: one composite economic index, evaluated with leave-one-cycle-out validation against a model without it. If it doesn't improve out-of-sample accuracy, it stays out.
